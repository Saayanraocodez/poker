Ground rules for exact work

  1. Rationals only. Every number in a proof is a fraction. Python's fractions.Fraction is your calculator; a decimal is a hint, never a step.
  2. Search with floats, decide with fractions. Floating point, LP solvers, MCCFR and SLSQP may find a candidate (a point, a multiplier, a direction). Only an exact check of that candidate counts.
  3. Check every computation two ways. The tools for this are zero-sum at every leaf, symmetry, and a second derivation (by deal table and by polynomial). Agreement between two methods that share no code is the project's standard; see the memory note on "two provers".
  4. sympy is a calculator, not an authority. Use it to expand and factor. If a step matters, re-verify it in Fractions or by hand.
  5. Keep a ledger. Write down what is proved, what is computed, and what is only observed, and never let a sentence of one kind cite a result of another.

Notation: \(\kappa = 1/24\) is the probability of each deal. A profile is \(p\in[0,1]^{48}\). Coordinate \(x_{jk}\) (\(x\in\{a,b,c\}\) for P1, P2, P3) is the probability of the aggressive action (bet with no bet out, call facing a bet) holding card \(j\) in situation \(k\).

Chapter 1. The game as a polynomial

1.1 Rules

Deck \(\{1,2,3,4\}\); each of P1, P2, P3 is dealt one card, which gives 24 equally likely deals. Everyone antes 1. One betting round, bet or call = 1 chip, no raises; P1 acts first, then P2, then P3. With no bet outstanding a player checks (K) or bets (B); facing a bet a player folds (F) or calls (C). The highest card among those who did not fold wins the pot. The sequences:

  • P1 bets: P2 answers, then P3 answers; end.
  • P1 checks, P2 bets: P3 answers, then P1 answers; end.
  • P1 and P2 check, P3 bets: P1 answers, then P2 answers; end.
  • All check: three-way showdown.

Read the table as follows: \(c_{33}\) is P3's probability of calling holding the 3 after "P1 bet, P2 folded"; \(b_{41}\) is P2's probability of betting the 4 after P1 checked.

1.2 Payoff at a leaf

At a terminal history, let \(s_i\) be what player \(i\) put in (1 ante, plus 1 if they bet or called). The pot is \(s_1+s_2+s_3\). The winner \(w\) is the highest card among non-folders. The payoff is \(-s_i\) for every player, plus the pot for \(w\). The three payoffs sum to zero at every leaf: chips are only moved, never created.

1.3 One deal, all 13 leaves

Deal \((1,2,4)\): P1 holds 1, P2 holds 2, P3 holds 4. Each leaf's probability, given the deal, is the product of the factors along its path. The aggressive action contributes \(x\), the passive one \((1-x)\):

{{leaves124}}

The seventh row is the SGS paper's worked leaf. P1 checks, P2 bets, P3 folds, P1 calls. P1 and P2 put in 2 each and P3 put in 1, so the pot is 5. P2's 2 beats P1's 1, so P2 nets \(5-2=+3\), P1 \(-2\), P3 \(-1\).

1.4 Expected utility

\[u_k(p)=\kappa\sum_{\text{24 deals}}\ \sum_{\text{13 leaves}}\Big(\prod_{\text{factors on the path}}\Big)\cdot \text{payoff}_k .\]

This is a polynomial in the 48 coordinates. On any path, a coordinate appears at most once, because a player acts at most once per information set on a path. So each term has degree at most 1 in every variable: \(u_k\) is multilinear. Everything in Chapter 2 follows from this one fact.

Proposition 1 (constant sum). \(u_1+u_2+u_3=0\) for every \(p\in[0,1]^{48}\). Proof. Sum the three payoffs leaf by leaf; each sum is 0. \(\square\) This is a property of the game, not of any equilibrium, so it cannot "fail a test". Write it as a structural fact.

Chapter 2. Multilinearity: the master key

Fix every coordinate but one, \(x\). A multilinear polynomial is then affine in \(x\):

\[u(\dots,x,\dots)=u(\dots,0,\dots)+x\big(u(\dots,1,\dots)-u(\dots,0,\dots)\big).\]

Four consequences are used everywhere:

  1. Exact derivatives from two evaluations: \(\partial u/\partial x = u(x{=}1)-u(x{=}0)\). No limits and no step sizes.
  2. Best replies are "bang-bang or indifferent": in one coordinate, a player either strictly prefers an endpoint or is exactly indifferent. That is why equilibrium conditions come in the form "interior \(\Rightarrow\) derivative = 0".
  3. Closed-form roots: \(\partial u/\partial x\) is itself affine in every other single coordinate, so "where does this derivative vanish?" has a closed-form answer. A pole is located by solving a linear equation, not by searching.
  4. Ratios of affine functions are Möbius maps. If \(N(c)\) and \(D(c)\) are affine in \(c\) with \(D(c^\ast)=0\) and \(N'=D'\), then \[\frac{N(c)}{D(c)}=\frac{N(c^\ast)+N'(c-c^\ast)}{D'(c-c^\ast)}=1+\frac{N(c^\ast)/D'}{c-c^\ast}.\] This identity is Theorem 2 (Chapter 6), once you know why \(N'=D'\).

Best responses split by card. Player \(k\)'s coordinates for card \(j\) appear only in the 6 deals where \(k\) holds \(j\). So \(\max_{\sigma_k}u_k\) splits into 4 independent problems, one per card, each over \(2^4\) pure plans: 64 exact evaluations per player. This is how an exact exploitability is computed (and why it can be done in Fractions).

Chapter 3. The incentive at one information set: the \(D\)-gradient

3.1 Definition

An information set \(i\) of player \(k\) is a pair (own card, public history). Its nodes are the deals consistent with it. For a node \(n\) write

  • \(R_{-k}(n)\): the product of chance and the other players' factors on the path to \(n\). Player \(k\)'s own earlier choices are left out; they are the same for every node of the set.
  • \(V_k(\cdot)\): \(k\)'s continuation value below a node under the profile.
\[D_i(p)=\kappa\sum_{n\in i}R_{-k}(n)\,\big(V_k(\text{aggressive child of }n)-V_k(\text{passive child of }n)\big),\] \[\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_i}=R_k(i)\,D_i .\]

Here \(R_k(i)\) is \(k\)'s own reach of the set. \(D_i\) is the incentive at the set, weighted by how likely the opponents are to bring play there. Dividing by \(\sum_n R_{-k}(n)\) gives the value difference under the beliefs the profile induces.

3.2 The six-deal recipe

  1. List the 6 deals in which the owner holds the card.
  2. For each, write the others' factor that brings play to this history.
  3. Compute the owner's continuation value after each action (often the play below is forced: use the profile).
  4. Multiply the difference by the reach factor, add the six rows, and multiply by \(\kappa\).

3.3 Worked example 1: \(D_{c_{33}}\), in full generality

P3 holds the 3 after "P1 bet, P2 folded". If P3 calls, the pot is \(2+1+2=5\); P3 put in 2 and wins against P1 iff \(3>\) P1's card, so P3 gets \(+3\) or \(-2\). If P3 folds, P3 gets \(-1\). P3 has no later decision.

3.5 Why the own reach is stripped (Lemma 1)

The plain gradient \(\partial u_k/\partial x_i=R_k(i)D_i\) vanishes whenever \(k\)'s own earlier move makes \(i\) unreachable, whatever happens there. A system of equations "\(\partial u_k/\partial x_i=0\) for interior coordinates" therefore admits profiles that are not equilibria; this was the project's erratum 8. With \(D\) instead:

Lemma 1. (a) If \(p\) is Nash, the D-condition holds at every own-reachable set: \[x_i>0\Rightarrow D_i\ge0,\qquad x_i<1\Rightarrow D_i\le 0.\] Re-choosing the own-unreached coordinates by backward induction gives a profile \(p'\) with the same play and payoffs that satisfies the D-condition at every set. (b) Conversely, if the D-condition holds everywhere, \(p\) is a Nash equilibrium.

Proof sketch (do it yourself). (b) Use backward induction over \(k\)'s own tree. At \(k\)'s last decisions the D-condition picks a maximiser of \(\sum_n R_{-k}(n)V_k(n)\); inductively every continuation is optimal, so \(p_k\) is a best reply. (a) At an own-reachable set \(R_k(i)>0\), so a profitable change of \(x_i\) alone would exist otherwise. For the re-choice, note that the changed sets have \(R_k=0\), so no reached node, payoff or other player's \(D\) changes. \(\square\)

5.1 The identities, in the order you can derive them

  1. \(b_{11}\) or \(b_{21}\) interior \(\Rightarrow\) \(a_{33}=\tfrac12\) (Chapter 3.4). The caller's frequency makes the bluffer indifferent: classic Kuhn logic.
  2. \(a_{33}\) interior \(\Rightarrow\) \(b_{41}=2(b_{11}+b_{21})\). The bettor's value-to-bluff ratio makes the caller indifferent.
  3. \(b_{33}\) interior \(\Rightarrow\) \(c_{11}+c_{21}=\tfrac12\). P3's total bluffing makes P2's call with the 3 indifferent.
  4. \(c_{11}\) interior \(\Rightarrow\) \(b_{33}=\tfrac12+b_{11}+\tfrac{b_{21}}2-b_{23}(1-b_{21})\) (after using step 2). \(c_{21}\) interior \(\Rightarrow\) \(b_{33}=\tfrac12+\tfrac{b_{11}}2+b_{21}\).
  5. Both interior (sub-family B) \(\Rightarrow\) equate them: \(\tfrac{b_{11}-b_{21}}{2}=b_{23}(1-b_{21})\). With \(b_{23}=0\) (forced unless \(c_{11}=\tfrac12\), by \(D_{b_{23}}=(2c_{11}-1)/12\)) this gives \(b_{11}=b_{21}\).
  6. So: \(c_{11}=0\) gives sub-family A (\(\beta=b_{21}\ge b_{11}\)), \(c_{11}=\tfrac12\) gives C (\(\beta=b_{11}\ge b_{21}\), \(b_{23}\) allowed), and in between is B (\(b_{11}=b_{21}\)). Table 3's single formula \(b_{33}=\tfrac12+\tfrac{b_{11}+b_{21}}2+\tfrac\beta2-b_{23}(1-b_{21})\) is these cases written with \(\beta=\max\{b_{11},b_{21}\}\).

  • \(D_{a_{11}}=D_{a_{21}}=-\tfrac16\big(c_{33}-(\tfrac12-b_{32})\big)\le0\iff c_{33}\ge\mathrm{lo}:=\tfrac12-b_{32}\).
  • \(D_{a_{41}}\le0\) gives the upper end, per sub-family:

Chapter 6. Deterrence windows, poles and the incidence ratio

6.1 Costs and the hard floor

Write \(\mathrm{cost}(a_{j1})=-\partial u_1/\partial a_{j1}\ge0\) for what P1 gives up by opening with card \(j\). From Chapter 5 (P1's own reach of the root is 1):

\[\mathrm{cost}(a_{11})=\mathrm{cost}(a_{21})=4\kappa(c_{33}-\mathrm{lo}),\qquad \mathrm{cost}(a_{41})=2\kappa(\mathrm{hi}-c_{33}),\qquad w:=\mathrm{hi}-\mathrm{lo}=\tfrac34(b_{11}+b_{21})+\tfrac\beta4 .\]

Proposition 5 (hard floor). \(w=0\iff b_{11}=b_{21}=0\iff\beta=0\iff\kappa\beta=0\). Proof: every term of \(w\) is non-negative. \(\square\) The window that leaves \(c_{33}\) free closes exactly when the SGS transfer \(\kappa\beta\) vanishes.

6.2 The incidence ratio and Theorem 2

For a deviation \(d=+e_{a_{j1}}\) by P1, \(\rho_{1\to3}=-\dfrac{\partial u_3/\partial a_{j1}}{\partial u_1/\partial a_{j1}}=\dfrac{N(c_{33})}{D(c_{33})}\). Both are affine in \(c_{33}\) (Chapter 2). Why is \(N'=D'\)? Look at the mixed second derivatives (computed exactly):

{{thm2_mixed}}

\(c_{33}\) acts only after P2 has folded, where P2's payoff is \(-1\) whatever P3 does. So \(\partial^2u_2/\partial a_{11}\partial c_{33}=0\), and by Proposition 1 the other two are negatives of each other: \(N'=-\partial^2u_3=\partial^2u_1=D'\). On the family (\(b_{22}=0\), \(b_{42}=1\)) this is \(D'=-\tfrac16=-4\kappa\). Chapter 2's Möbius identity then gives

\[\rho(c_{33})=1+\frac{R}{c_{33}-c_{33}^\ast},\qquad R=\frac{N(c_{33}^\ast)}{D'}=\frac{t}{D'} .\]

At \(c_{33}^\ast\) the deviation costs P1 nothing, so by Proposition 1 it moves exactly \(t=\partial u_2=-\partial u_3\) between the other two: the residue is the costless transfer divided by the mixed second derivative. This is exact, not asymptotic.

6.3 A ratio that never moves: \(\rho_{2\to1}(+e_{b_{31}})=\tfrac35\)

{{ex35}}

With \(c_{11}+c_{21}=\tfrac12\) (Chapter 5, step 3) these become \(\tfrac1{16}\) and \(-\tfrac5{48}\) whatever the other parameters are. So \(\rho=-\frac{1/16}{-5/48}=\tfrac35\) on the entire family. PAPER_KIT §9 does the same computation as a six-deal table; do it both ways.

Chapter 7. Beliefs, trembles and refinements

7.1 Beliefs from trembles

At an unreached set both \(D_v\) and its reach \(R_v\) vanish, so Nash says nothing there. Perturb: put \(x_w=\varepsilon r_w\) where the profile plays 0 and \(x_w=1-\varepsilon r_w\) where it plays 1. Then \(D_v=\varepsilon^{m}A_v(r)+O(\varepsilon^{m+1})\), and the sign of the leading coefficient \(A_v\) is the sign of the value difference under the belief the trembles induce. This is Kreps–Wilson consistency in closed form. For \(c_{33}\) the leading term is Chapter 3's formula with \(a_{j1}\) replaced by \(r_{a_{j1}}\):

\[A_{c_{33}}(r)=\kappa\big(4r_{a_{11}}+4r_{a_{21}}-2r_{a_{41}}\big)\quad(b_{12}=b_{22}=0,\ b_{42}=1).\]

Normalise per information set. Each leading coefficient is homogeneous in the trembles of one set's predecessors. Normalising all ratios on one global simplex lets a whole group vanish, and the form then reads \(0=0\); 28 patterns were undecidable that way and died instantly per set.

7.3 Binary sets (Lemma R1)

Lemma R1. When every information set has two actions, a completely mixed profile is \(\varepsilon\)-proper (van Damme's extensive-form sense) iff it is \(\varepsilon'\)-perfect with \(\varepsilon'=\varepsilon/(1-\varepsilon)\). Proof: with two actions, "inferior action \(\le\varepsilon\cdot\) better action" and "inferior action \(\le\varepsilon\)" differ by the factor \(1-\varepsilon\le\sigma(\text{better})\le1\). \(\square\) So extensive-form proper = extensive-form perfect = agent-normal-form perfect here.

7.4 Normal-form properness selects \(c_{33}=\mathrm{lo}+w/3\) (Theorem 3)

Properness orders trembles by cost: a costlier action trembles infinitely less often.

  • If \(\mathrm{cost}(a_{11})<\mathrm{cost}(a_{41})\), then \(r_4/(r_1+r_2)\to0\), so \(q\to1>\tfrac15\) and P3 strictly calls: \(c_{33}=1\), which lies outside the window (\(\mathrm{hi}\le\tfrac{15}{16}\)).
  • If \(\mathrm{cost}(a_{11})>\mathrm{cost}(a_{41})\), then \(q\to0\) and P3 strictly folds: \(c_{33}=0<\mathrm{lo}\) when \(b_{32}<\tfrac12\).
  • Hence an interior \(c_{33}\) needs equal costs: \(4(c_{33}-\mathrm{lo})=2(\mathrm{hi}-c_{33})\), i.e. \(c_{33}=\mathrm{lo}+w/3\). There the knife-edge \(r_4=2(r_1+r_2)\) is admissible.

The assumption to state. This is properness on the normal form, where whole pure strategies are ordered by cost and the behavioural rates \(r_j\) inherit the order. Agent-form properness would not compare \(a_{11}\) with \(a_{41}\), which belong to different information sets.

7.5 Perfection and quasi-perfection: the first-order test

Take a curve \(x(t)=\sigma+t\delta+O(t^2)\) of completely mixed profiles: \(\delta_v>0\) where \(\sigma_v=0\) and \(\delta_v<0\) where \(\sigma_v=1\). Along it, the equilibrium must stay a best reply:

  • Interior coordinates must stay exactly indifferent: \(\nabla D_v(\sigma)\cdot\delta=0\) to first order, and then exactly by the implicit function theorem if the indifference Jacobian has full rank.
  • Pure coordinates with \(D_v(\sigma)=0\) (unreached or tight) must get the right sign at first order, or be sign-definite on the whole cube.
  • Strict pure coordinates need nothing at first order.

The constraints are linear in \(\delta\), so this is an LP; infeasibility comes with an exact Farkas certificate. Perfection uses the full gradient: a player's own other trembles count. Quasi-perfection (van Damme 1984) evaluates each player's incentives at \((\sigma_k,x_{-k})\): own later moves are taken from \(\sigma\) itself. In the LP this simply deletes each row's own-player columns.

New result (today, qperf.py). On Theorem 6.5's slice the certificate succeeds for every \(c_{33}\) in the open interval \((\tfrac12,\tfrac{23}{32})\), for both refinements. The slice is leaf 469, \(b_{11}=b_{21}=\tfrac18\), \(c_{11}=\tfrac14\), refined off-path values. The proof:

  • One direction for all \(c_{33}\). The direction \(\delta\) found at \(c_{33}=\tfrac9{16}\) works for all \(c_{33}\). For quasi-perfection it has \(\delta_{a_{11}}=\tfrac37\), \(\delta_{a_{21}}=\tfrac1{14}\), \(\delta_{a_{41}}=1\), which is the knife-edge \(r_4=2(r_1+r_2)\).
  • Signs checked exactly. Every condition becomes a polynomial in \(c_{33}\) whose sign is decided on the interval by exact real-root isolation.
  • Full rank. An \(8\times8\) minor of the indifference Jacobian has the constant determinant \(-\tfrac{95}{3439853568}\neq0\).

The endpoints are the two poles and stay undecided at first order. So quasi-perfection, like extensive-form properness, leaves \(c_{33}\) an interval. Only normal-form properness picks \(\tfrac{55}{96}\).

Chapter 8. Certificates: how a computer proof is checked, and how to check one by hand

A computer proof here is never "the program said so". It is a certificate: a finite object that an independent, solver-free checker verifies with Fractions. You can verify a small one by hand, and that is the best way to understand the big ones.

For example, constraint 4 says \(b_{33}=\tfrac{1+b_{41}}2\ge\tfrac12\) because \(b_{41}\ge0\). The checker (checkcert.py) re-derives exactly this, and accepts a claimed bound only if it is no stronger than what it derived.

8.2 LP certificates (weak duality)

When contraction stalls, a node is closed by an LP relaxation. Polynomial terms are replaced by new variables with valid linear bounds (the McCormick envelope: for \(z=xy\) on a box, four linear inequalities). The certificate is a vector of multipliers \(y\ge0\) with \(A^\top y+w\ge c\) and \(b\cdot y+U\cdot w\le1\). Weak duality then bounds the slack variable and shows that no point satisfies every strict inequality. To check one: rebuild each row from its stated origin, check the signs of the multipliers, and do one matrix–vector product in Fractions.

Toy example. To show that \(x\ge\tfrac12\), \(y\ge\tfrac12\), \(x+y\le\tfrac34\) has no solution, take multipliers \(1,1,1\): adding the rows gives \(0\ge\tfrac14\), a contradiction. Every LP certificate in the project is this, with more rows.

8.3 Identity certificates

"On leaf 62, \(b_{21}=b_{41}/2\)" is certified by writing the left side minus the right as a combination of the leaf's equations: here \(b_{21}-\tfrac{b_{41}}2=6\,D_{a_{33}}|_{\text{leaf}}\). In general \(h=\sum_k q_k e_k\), checked by expanding both sides.

8.4 Existence by interval Newton (Krawczyk): the (3,5) equilibrium

For \(G:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^n\), a box \(Y\) with midpoint \(m\), a fixed matrix \(C\approx G'(m)^{-1}\) and an interval enclosure \(G'(Y)\) of the Jacobian over \(Y\), set

\[K(Y)=m-C\,G(m)+(I-C\,G'(Y))(Y-m).\]

If \(K(Y)\subset\operatorname{int}Y\), then \(G\) has exactly one zero in \(Y\). To check the certificate, compute \(G(m)\) and the interval matrix in exact rationals, multiply, and compare endpoints. Every pure coordinate's \(D\) must then be shown strictly signed on all of \(Y\) by exact interval evaluation.

8.5 Lagrangian (correlated-equilibrium) certificates: no P1-silent (3,5) equilibrium

Goal: every equilibrium of a restricted game has \(V_5<\tfrac{19}8\). Choose \(\mu_\tau\ge0\), one per deviation plan \(\tau\) of an agent \(a\), and set

\[L(s)=u_1(s\mid5)+\sum_\tau\mu_\tau\big(u_a(s)-u_a(\tau,s_{-a})\big).\]

At a Nash equilibrium every bracket is \(\ge0\), so \(V_5\le L(s)\le\max L\). Since \(L\) is multilinear, \(\max L\) is attained at a pure profile, and a finite exhaustive check over \(2^{40}\) pure profiles gives \(\max L=\tfrac{28071283}{12000000}<\tfrac{19}8\). Two independent checkers agree. To check it yourself, convince yourself of the three inequalities, then audit one checker.

8.6 Distance certificates (today's R14 re-read)

For a polyhedral superset \(\{Ax=b,\ l\le x\le u\}\) of the Nash set and a point \(p\): for any \(y\), \(\lambda,\mu\ge0\), put \(g=A^\top y+\lambda-\mu\). Then

\[\|x-p\|_\infty\ \ge\ \frac{y\cdot(b-Ap)+\lambda\cdot(l-p)-\mu\cdot(u-p)}{\|g\|_1}.\]

The simplest instance: if every equilibrium has \(a_{32}=0\), then \(\|x-p\|_\infty\ge p_{a_{32}}\). That one-liner carries the certificate for 78 of the 150 MCCFR outputs.

Chapter 9. The big theorems: what a person can check, and what needs the machine

9.1 The proof of Theorem 4, as a chain

  1. Lemma 1 (pen and paper): it suffices to find all profiles satisfying the D-condition everywhere.
  2. Labels (Lemmas 2–4, pen and paper): label each coordinate 0, 1 or MIX. The \(3^{48}\) cells partition the cube; a node of the enumeration fixes some labels; pruning a node is sound if its system (Lemma 3) is infeasible.
  3. The root: P1's four openings give 81 cells. Three (\(a_{11}=a_{21}=0\), \(a_{41}=1\)) are killed at the root by exact certificates; 78 survive.
  4. The 77 betting cells are closed by certificate trees: 4,394,323 nodes for 71 cells (checkenum.py) and 26,223 for the last 6 (checkenum2.py, an independent implementation); 0 failures.
  5. The silent cell (\(a_{j1}=0\) for all \(j\)) yields 668 supports. 656 are certified empty and 12 are FAMILY leaves (Appendix C), whose systems are exactly Chapter 5's identities and inequalities.
  6. Lemma 5 (pen and paper): a FAMILY leaf's points agree with a family member on every reached coordinate.

9.2 What you can do by hand

  • Lemmas 1–5 and Lemma R1, completely.
  • The twelve FAMILY systems: re-derive their identities as in Chapter 5 (Appendix C lists them).
  • Any single certificate: pick a few from nefull_certs.json.gz and cert_*.json.gz of increasing size, as in 8.1.
  • The three root kills: show directly that if P1 never bluffs and always bets the 4, someone has a profitable deviation.

9.3 What needs the machine, and how to earn trust in it

  • Audit the checkers, not the provers. They are short, use only Fractions, and import only the tree.
  • Re-run recheck_all.py, checkenum2.py and the (3,5) checkers on your own machine.
  • Mutation test: tamper with certificates and confirm rejection (the project did 2,463 such controls, all rejected).
  • Write your own checker for one certificate type from this workbook's descriptions alone. Agreement between two people's checkers is stronger than one person's audit.

9.4 A research problem for a human-sized proof

The (3,5) "no P1-silent equilibrium" proof is a certificate a person can understand: 24 multipliers and one exhaustive maximum. Is there a similar short certificate for "P1 never bets at (3,4)"? It would replace millions of tree nodes with a few dozen deviation inequalities. The method is the one in §8.5: bound P1's gain from betting over the equilibrium set by incentive constraints.
